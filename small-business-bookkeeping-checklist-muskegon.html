Why the routine matters

Organized records help with more than filing a tax return.

The IRS explains that good records can help a business monitor progress, prepare financial statements, identify the source of receipts, track deductible expenses, prepare tax returns, and support items reported on those returns.

01

Understand the business

Current books make it easier to see whether revenue is growing, which expenses are increasing, and whether the business is actually producing profit.

02

Prepare cleaner reports

Reliable Profit and Loss reports and Balance Sheets start with transactions that have been entered, categorized, and reconciled consistently.

03

Reduce tax-time scrambling

When income, expenses, assets, and supporting documents are already organized, year-end tax preparation starts from a much better place.

The monthly checklist

Seven bookkeeping checkpoints to review before another month closes.

You do not need to wait until December to find out something is missing. Use these checkpoints monthly, then adjust the process as the business grows.

01

Keep business and personal activity separate

Use dedicated business banking whenever practical and avoid mixing personal purchases into the business account. The IRS specifically notes that opening a separate business checking account is a strong early recordkeeping step.

02

Record every source of business income

Do not rely only on year-end Forms 1099. Match deposits, invoices, card payments, cash receipts, and other income sources to the business records.

03

Enter and categorize expenses

Record expenses while they are still recognizable. Keep enough detail to understand what was purchased, why it was business-related, and which account paid it.

04

Reconcile bank and credit-card accounts

Compare the books to each statement. Reconciliation helps catch duplicate entries, missing transactions, incorrect amounts, and items posted to the wrong account.

05

Save supporting documents

Invoices, receipts, paid bills, deposit records, account statements, and other supporting documents help explain and substantiate entries in the books.

06

Track equipment, vehicles, and worker payments

Keep purchase details for business assets and maintain appropriate records for vehicle use, contractors, employees, payroll, and other worker-related transactions.

07

Review the reports before moving on

Look at revenue, major expenses, cash balances, liabilities, and unusual changes. A monthly review is more useful when the numbers lead to questions while the details are still fresh.

Signs the books need attention

If several of these sound familiar, cleanup may be the better first step.

A

Months are missing

Statements exist, but transactions have not been entered or reconciled for several months.

B

Personal and business are mixed

Business expenses are spread across personal cards, business accounts, cash, and payment apps without a consistent record.

C

The reports do not make sense

Profit looks unrealistic, account balances do not match statements, or expenses are sitting in large miscellaneous categories.

Bookkeeping questions

A few common questions from small-business owners.

How often should I update my bookkeeping?

A consistent monthly process is a practical baseline for many small businesses. Businesses with higher transaction volume, frequent payroll, inventory, or faster reporting needs may benefit from a more frequent routine.

Do I need bookkeeping software?

Not every business needs the same system. The IRS generally allows a recordkeeping system suited to the business as long as it clearly shows income and expenses. The important part is that the process is complete, consistent, and supported by records.

What if my books are already several months behind?

Start with catch-up or cleanup rather than pretending the current month is the only issue. Allegiant can review how far behind the records are, which accounts are involved, and what source documents are available before confirming the scope.
