Muskegon small-business guide

Behind on your business books? Start with a clear catch-up plan.

A practical guide for contractors, salons, and service businesses in Muskegon, North Muskegon, and West Michigan.

By Kyle Mura, owner of Allegiant Tax Preparation · November 10, 2026

If you run a small business in Muskegon or West Michigan, you already know how it goes. The work comes first. The receipts pile up in a bag, the bank statements sit unopened in your email, and the books slip a month, then three, then a year. Falling behind does not mean you have to sort out the entire backlog before asking for help.

I'm Kyle Mura, the owner of Allegiant Tax Preparation in North Muskegon. Below is how I think about getting caught up, what the difference is between catch-up, cleanup and ongoing bookkeeping, and what you can gather before you reach out.

Catch-up, cleanup and ongoing work are not the same thing

These three services get mixed together, so it helps to separate them.

Catch-up bookkeeping brings recent months into order. This usually means organizing the records you have, entering and categorizing transactions, and reconciling the bank and credit-card accounts that have fallen behind.

Cleanup and correction is for books that have problems that need fixing first. Balances may not match the bank, categories may be inconsistent, or earlier entries may need to be corrected before the reports can be trusted.

Ongoing monthly bookkeeping is the routine that keeps the books current once they are in order. It typically covers transaction organization, monthly reconciliations and reports based on the scope we agree on.

Ongoing monthly bookkeeping starts at $199 per month. Catch-up and cleanup are one-time services, and they are quoted separately. I review the records first, then give you a price. The final number depends on how many months are behind, the number of bank and credit-card accounts, the transaction volume, the condition of the records and the reports you need.

What to gather before you reach out

You do not need everything perfect to start. A short list of what you have is enough for me to see where things stand:

  • Bank statements for each business checking and savings account
  • Credit-card statements for each business card
  • Any payment processor reports, such as card processor or invoicing platform exports
  • Your sales records or invoices, if you issue them
  • Receipts and bills you have kept, even if they are not sorted
  • Payroll reports, if you have employees
  • The bookkeeping software you currently use, if any; authorized accountant access can be arranged separately without sharing your password

If you have a shoebox of paper, that is fine. Tell me it is there.

Bank deposits are not automatically income

Treating every bank deposit as income can give you a misleading picture of the business. A deposit into your business account might be a customer payment, but it could also be a loan, an owner contribution, a transfer from another account, or a refund. Each one is recorded differently.

When I reconcile an account, I match each deposit and payment to a source document where I can. If the source is unclear, I note it and ask you, rather than guessing. That helps keep your profit and loss report accurate and makes it much easier to answer questions later.

What a catch-up project should produce

Before work starts, agree on which months and accounts will be addressed, what records you will provide, and what you will receive when the work is complete. Depending on the scope, that may include reconciled accounts, corrected categories, financial reports and a list of open questions that still need your input.

Ask how missing information will be handled. An unidentified transfer or an incomplete processor report should be flagged, rather than quietly treated as a final number. You should also know which periods are complete and which are still waiting on records. That makes the handoff into ongoing monthly bookkeeping much clearer.

For contractors, salons and other service businesses, a workable monthly routine matters as much as clearing the old backlog. Decide who gathers receipts, who provides statements, and how questions will be answered. The goal is a process you can maintain alongside running the business.

Questions to ask any bookkeeper

Whether you work with me or someone else, these questions are worth asking before you agree to anything:

  1. What is included in the monthly price, and what is quoted separately?
  2. Is catch-up or cleanup work included, or is it a separate one-time quote?
  3. How will you review my records before you give me a price?
  4. Which reports will I receive each month?
  5. Who will I actually work with, and how do I reach them?
  6. How are my financial records stored and shared?

A good bookkeeper should be able to answer each of these in plain language.
